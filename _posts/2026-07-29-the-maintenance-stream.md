*A sequel to the gates argument, and its bill: every correction that migrates into a mechanism leaves behind a setting someone has to keep honest, and no productivity account is pricing that work.*

In [the previous essay](https://jongsun.dev/gates-not-reminders/) I argued that a correction which has to be remembered is not yet a correction, and that the fix for a recurring defect is a mechanism, a gate that fires before the tool call and blocks, rather than a better-written rule. That argument stands, and the defects those gates guard against have not recurred. What the essay did not price is what happens after the gate is written. A gate watches a pattern, and patterns get renamed. When that happens the gate still loads on every call, matches nothing, and reports no error, because a gate that never fires is indistinguishable from a gate whose defect never recurs. Nothing in the mechanism tells you which of the two you have. You can walk past an unlocked door for a month, crediting the lock.

I can put numbers on the stock this produces, because mine is a directory I can count. It holds 269 persisted rules. Most of them are recent, 190 last revised within the month, which is what a practice looks like while it is still writing rules faster than it is auditing them. Forty-two have not been touched in over three months. Seven cite a file path that no longer resolves, which is the cheapest possible staleness check and the only one I have ever run against the whole set. What the other 262 assert about a workflow that may have moved underneath them, I do not know, because nothing checks.

## Where the interventions go

A maturing practice moves its corrections out of the conversation and into standing structures. In my own setup those structures now include the two gates, a store of persisted rules that load into every session, routing policies deciding which class of task goes to which agent, approval scopes, category definitions frozen so that a recurring report stays comparable across weeks, expiry windows on certification markers, and exit criteria for verification loops. Call these owned parameters: settings whose criterion a human writes and revises, and which then govern many episodes without further conversation. Every tool I adopt adds a few more, because every tool arrives with defaults that turn out to be wrong for my work in some specific way, and each such correction becomes another parameter.

The per-episode economics of this migration are as good as they look. The commit defect that once drew a correction every few sessions now costs nothing per episode, because one gate governs every episode. Divide the cost of oversight by the number of tasks it covers and the quotient falls toward zero as the denominator grows. If that quotient were the whole story, working with agents would be an activity in which the human labor simply evaporates, and the only open question would be how fast.

## The component that resists

Economics has an old account of why some costs refuse to evaporate. When one component of an activity enjoys fast productivity growth and a complementary component does not, the resistant component's share of total cost rises even as its absolute volume falls, which is how the labor-intensive corners of an economy come to dominate budgets while the automated corners shrink toward free. Applied naively to agent oversight, that account would predict that the resistant component is the message stream, the live human steering that no mechanism replaces. The naive application points exactly backwards, because the message stream is precisely what the migration automates away. The component that resists is the maintenance of the parameter stock that the migration creates.

Owned parameters age, and they age silently in both directions. A gate that stops firing protects nothing, and that failure announces itself only when the defect it guarded recurs, if anyone is still attributing recurrences to particular gates by then. A gate that fires on a case it was never meant to catch does announce itself, as friction, but the announcement teaches the wrong lesson: the fastest way past your own gate is to reword the input until it passes, which is the bypass the gate exists to prevent, rehearsed by its author. A parameter can also stay functional while its subject dissolves. A rule that encodes the ordering constraints between two release processes is correct on the day it is written and becomes fiction the day those processes merge, and nothing about it changes in the interim. It still loads into every session, costs nothing visible, and waits to misdirect the first decision that leans on it. The seven broken paths in my own store are the visible tail of that class, visible only because a path is the one thing a script can check. A rule that names a process rather than a file fails the same way and leaves no trace a script can find.

The asymmetry that makes the stock grow is blunt. Writing a new parameter takes minutes, and the minutes are spent at the moment of maximum motivation, right after the defect burned me. Auditing the existing stock, walking every parameter and asking whether the hazard it encodes still exists in the shape the parameter assumes, is a day's work across 269 of them that buys nothing new, only the removal of things that should no longer have been there. That audit does not get cheaper as agent capability improves, because each parameter has to be checked against the current state of a workflow that drifts independently of every other workflow. The cost of keeping the stock honest grows linearly with the size of the stock, while the cost of growing the stock stays constant and small.

## The line item that does not exist

Now put the two motions together and look at what a productivity account records. At adoption time the account books a gain, since a recurring correction became a mechanism and per-episode oversight fell toward zero. The maintenance of the resulting parameter goes unbooked, because no ledger has a line for it. There is a line item, implicitly, for writing the gate, in the session where the writing happened. There is no line item for confirming, next quarter, that the gate still watches something that exists. An account that books the gain and prices the maintenance at zero overstates the gain, and the overstatement is not bounded by anything, because the unpriced stock grows with every migration. The error compounds at exactly the rate the practice matures.

Two numbers from my own record. I keep a coded log of what my input to coding agents actually carries, classed intervention by intervention, and in that log about eight percent of classed interventions were aimed at the rule store rather than at the task in front of me, writing or revising standing structures rather than steering live work. That is the flow. The 269 rules are the stock it has accumulated. One record, one practitioner, one workflow, so both are anchors rather than estimates. The anchor is enough to establish the category. Whatever the true share is across practices, it is not zero, and it is aimed at exactly the layer that a per-episode measure cannot see.

## Still a good trade, not a free one

None of this argues against the gates, and the previous essay's test survives its own bill: if a correction has recurred after the rule's best rewrite, the next revision should still not be prose. What the bill changes is the description of the trade. A mechanism does not delete the cost of a recurring correction. A mechanism converts a large recurring per-episode cost into a smaller recurring maintenance cost, and that conversion is a good trade on any horizon I can see, but a trade is not a gift. The trade stays good only for as long as someone keeps the stock honest, and keeping the stock honest is work, schedulable, budgetable, assignable work that currently carries no name in any process I have seen, including my own. The work that survives automation is the work of maintaining the things that did the automating. I would rather have that work than the corrections it replaced. I would also rather it appeared in the accounting, because work that appears nowhere eventually stops being done, and a stock nobody audits fails silently, in both directions, while everyone credits the lock.
