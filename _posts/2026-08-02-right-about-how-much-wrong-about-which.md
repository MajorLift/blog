title: "Right About How Much, Wrong About Which"
date: 2026-08-02 00:00:00 +0000
---

*Five measuring instruments in one day, each returning plausible numbers about one person's corrections to a coding agent, and each wrong in a different place. The fifth passed every validation that read its distribution and failed the one that read its labels, and the sequence of failures ended up worth more than any number in the notebook.*

One purpose, held all day. I keep a log of my own corrections to an AI coding agent, and I wanted to know which kind repeats, on a theory that repetition marks something an agent has not absorbed. The coding scheme sorts corrections into three classes, preference, fact-checking, and reframing. Whether the classes differ in repetition rate was the question, and for most of the day they appeared to differ by a wide margin. By evening that appearance was dead. Five instruments had failed in five distinguishable places, and the record of those failures became the finding, since every number I still trust came out of catching an instrument rather than running one.

The first instrument was a classifier keyed on keywords in a message body, and it sorted 2,205 inputs into three classes for the cost of a glance. Its defect was not a matter of tuning. Verification vocabulary is this corpus's subject matter, since my corrections are largely about evidence and proof standards, so the words that classifier took as signals of fact-checking were the topic under discussion rather than a signal within it. One record titled "Running the machinery is not a finding" scored as fact-checking because evidence appears eleven times beneath that title. It is a reframing, a correction of what counts as a result rather than of any fact.

The second was a detector built to scale a hand-coding up, and against that hand-coding it agreed at Cohen's kappa 0.288, misreading one category as another in six of eight cases. I discarded it. The third was an automatic extractor of rules banning a specific word, and it returned 164 candidate tests, nearly all of them any quoted phrase sitting near a negation. I discarded that too and kept six hand-verified rules. Both had returned plausible numbers, since 164 looks like a harvest and any kappa looks like a measurement, and both numbers described the shape of my text rather than the property I was after.

The fourth instrument was a detector meant to find corrections, and it found the tooling instead. Its six most-duplicated corrections were three re-fires of one automated prompt, a message from another agent, and two command templates, all arriving on the same channel a person types into. The channel records what came through and not who composed it, so a detector reading the channel attributes the machinery's repetitions to the person. Filtering the machinery out moved the measured duplication rate from 58 percent to 16.4 percent. Most of what this instrument had reported as my repetitiveness was infrastructure firing on schedule.

The fifth instrument lasted long enough to produce a headline. A marker-based classifier reported that preference corrections repeat 35.3 percent of the time against 10.8 and 6.2 for the other two, and a gap that size would have shown that what a principal wants is learnable from their own record. That was the essay this essay was going to be. Two controls left the figure intact. A third control was length, and under it the classifier came apart: its preference items ran to a median of 249 words against 24 for fact-checking, so it had been sorting by message length and calling the long tail preference. Within a matched band those rates came out at 3.6 and 4.5 percent. Its ordering did not merely shrink under control, it reversed.

What replaced the headline is smaller and holds up. Three fresh model instances, carrying none of the conversation that produced the corpus, coded 100 messages blind against the classification criterion, and they agreed among themselves at Fleiss kappa 0.734. Their consensus supplied what no instrument in that sequence had, a set of labels formed apart from this corpus's own vocabulary, so my marker classifier could finally be graded item by item. Against consensus it recovered corrections at 73 percent precision and 68 percent recall. Among the 65 corrections the panel agreed on, it matched the class 12 times, with its 45 abstentions scored as errors. On the 20 messages where it committed to a class, the class matched 12 times. Where it commits, then, its failure is coverage rather than accuracy, and those two failures call for different repairs: more markers for one, a different instrument for the other.

Item-level grading is what caught it, because the marginals had concealed the whole condition. The broken classifier's corpus-wide share for preference and the blind panel's share of corrections both sat near a third, so any validation reading the distribution rather than the individual labels would have passed the instrument. I had been reading distributions all day, since that is what an instrument prints when asked how a corpus looks. Right about how much and wrong about which: nothing in its output could separate those two conditions, because a marginal is exactly the statistic that survives arbitrary reshuffling of labels underneath. Only laying its labels beside independently formed labels for the same items separates them. The independence is what carries the weight, since four of the five instruments failed precisely by inheriting the corpus's vocabulary or the tooling's traffic as if either were me.

One thread from the day generalizes past instruments, and it opened as a doubt about a single number. The number counted how often I confer permission, and the doubt was whether the agent's own choice of when to ask for confirmation pollutes the count. The doubt holds and widens. A conferral of permission exists only in response to a request, so its frequency is a joint product of the agent's asking policy and the person's disposition to grant, and only the disposition is a property of the person. My corpus bears that out at scale: across 22,448 agent turns, how often a turn invites a response runs from 11.5 to 19.2 percent across four models, and those rates do not track capability ordering. A frequency measured on one side of an exchange is a joint product of both sides, and an instrument pointed at one side has measured the pair whether or not the analysis says so.

None of this is claimed past one person's corpus and one day. What that day left behind is six hand-verified rules, a duplication rate of 16.4 percent, a dead headline about preferences, a panel consensus at 0.734, and a classifier whose failure now has a location. What I set out to find was an artifact of message length. The five instruments meant to deliver it delivered instead a catalog of the ways a plausible number gets made, and that catalog is what I kept.
