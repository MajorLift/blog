title: "Right About How Much, Wrong About Which"
date: 2026-08-02 00:00:00 +0000
---

*Five measuring instruments in one day, each returning plausible numbers about one person's corrections to a coding agent, and each wrong in a different place. The fifth passed every validation that read its distribution and failed the one that read its labels, and the sequence of failures ended up worth more than any number in the notebook.*

The day had one purpose. I keep a log of my own corrections to an AI coding agent, and I wanted to know which kind of correction repeats, on the theory that a correction that repeats marks something the agent has not absorbed. The coding scheme sorts corrections into three classes, preference, fact-checking, and reframing, and the question was whether the classes differ in their repetition rates. They appeared to, and by a wide margin, for most of the day. By evening the appearance was dead, five instruments had failed in five distinguishable places, and the record of the failures had become the finding, since every number the day produced that I still trust came out of catching an instrument rather than out of running one.

The first instrument was a classifier keyed on keywords in the message body, and it sorted the corpus into the three classes at a glance's cost. Its defect was not a matter of tuning. Verification vocabulary is this corpus's subject matter, because the corrections are largely about evidence and proof standards, so the words the classifier took as signals of fact-checking are the topic under discussion rather than a signal within the discussion. A record titled "Running the machinery is not a finding" scored as fact-checking because the word evidence appears eleven times beneath the title, and the record is a reframing, a correction of what counts as a result rather than of any fact.

The second instrument was a detector built to scale a hand-coding up, and against the hand-coding it agreed at Cohen's kappa 0.288, misreading one category as another in six of eight cases. I discarded the detector. The third was an automatic extractor of rules that ban a specific word, and it returned 164 candidate tests, nearly all of them any quoted phrase sitting near a negation. I discarded the extractor and kept six hand-verified rules. Both instruments had returned plausible numbers, since a count of 164 looks like a harvest and any kappa looks like a measurement, and both numbers described the shape of the text rather than the property I was after.

The fourth instrument was a detector meant to find corrections, and it found the tooling instead. Its six most-duplicated corrections were three re-fires of one automated prompt, a message from another agent, and two command templates, all of them arriving on the same channel a person types into. The channel records what came through, not who composed what came through, so a detector reading the channel attributes the machinery's repetitions to the person. Filtering the machinery out moved the measured duplication rate from 58 percent to 16.4 percent, which means most of what the instrument had been reporting as my repetitiveness was the infrastructure firing on schedule.

The fifth instrument lasted long enough to produce a headline. A marker-based classifier for the three classes reported that preference corrections repeat 35.3 percent of the time, against 10.8 and 6.2 for the other two classes, and a gap of that size would have shown that what a principal wants is learnable from the principal's own record. That was the essay this essay was going to be. Two controls left the figure intact. The third control was length, and under length the classifier came apart, because its preference items ran to a median of 249 words against 24 for the fact-checking class, so the classifier had been sorting by message length and calling the long tail preference. Within a matched length band the rates came out at 3.6 and 4.5 percent, and the ordering the classifier had produced did not merely shrink under the control but reversed.

What replaced the headline is smaller and holds up. Three fresh model instances, carrying none of the conversation that produced the corpus, coded 100 messages blind against the classification criterion, and they agreed among themselves at Fleiss kappa 0.734. Their consensus supplied what no instrument in the sequence had, a set of labels formed apart from the corpus's own vocabulary, so the marker classifier could finally be graded item by item. Against the consensus the classifier recovered corrections at 73 percent precision and 68 percent recall. Among the 65 corrections the panel agreed on, the classifier matched the class 12 times, with its 45 abstentions scored as errors, and on the 20 messages where the classifier committed to a class, the class matched 12 times. The failure was coverage rather than accuracy where the classifier committed, and the two failures call for different repairs, more markers for the first and a different instrument for the second.

Item-level grading is what caught it, because the marginals had concealed the whole condition. The broken classifier's corpus-wide share for the preference class and the blind panel's share of corrections both sat near a third, so any validation that read the distribution rather than the individual labels would have passed the instrument. I had been reading distributions all day, since a distribution is what an instrument prints when asked how the corpus looks. The classifier was right about how much and wrong about which, and nothing in its output could separate those two conditions, because a marginal is exactly the statistic that survives arbitrary reshuffling of the labels underneath. Only laying the classifier's labels beside independently formed labels for the same items could separate them, and the independence carries the weight, since four of the five instruments failed precisely by inheriting the corpus's vocabulary or the tooling's traffic as if either were the person.

One thread from the day generalizes past instruments, and it opened as a doubt about a single number. The number counted how often I confer permission, and the doubt was whether the agent's own choice of when to ask for confirmation pollutes the count. The doubt holds and widens. A conferral of permission exists only in response to a request, so the frequency of conferrals is a joint product of the agent's asking policy and the person's disposition to grant, and only the disposition is a property of the person. The corpus bears the point out at scale: across 22,448 agent turns, the rate at which a turn invites a response runs from 11.5 to 19.2 percent across four models, and the rates do not track the models' capability ordering. A frequency measured on one side of an exchange is a joint product of both sides, and an instrument pointed at one side has measured the pair whether or not the analysis says so.

None of this is claimed past one person's corpus and one day. What the day left behind is six hand-verified rules, a duplication rate of 16.4 percent, a dead headline about preferences, a panel consensus at 0.734, and a classifier whose failure now has a location. The finding I set out for was an artifact of message length. The five instruments that were supposed to deliver the finding delivered a catalog of the ways a plausible number gets made, and the catalog is what I kept.
