*You ask what you are missing, and the asking half-works. The half that fails is structural, and no rephrasing of the question repairs it.*

Near the end of any serious piece of work comes the familiar move. You turn to the model, or to a colleague, and ask what you are missing. The question is the standard remedy for blind spots, and it half-works. The half that works is easy to describe, since a capable responder can volunteer considerations you never raised, and sometimes does. The half that fails is structural. It fails the same way for the model and for the colleague, and it fails under every phrasing, because the failure sits in the act of requesting rather than in the words.

A request specifies what it seeks. That property is not a defect of phrasing but the definition of a request: a description, composed in advance, of the output that would satisfy it. The trouble starts there, because a possibility you have not represented cannot appear in any description you compose. To ask for it by name, you would have to represent it, and once you can represent it, it is no longer the thing you are missing. So the thing you most need is precisely the thing no request can specify. It arrives obliquely, as a volunteered aside, a tangent, an answer to a question you did not ask, or it does not arrive at all. One qualification the claim needs, because it is easy to over-read. What cannot be specified is the content. The relation can be: asking for the reading your framing forecloses names no possibility while demanding one, and shifts the representing onto whoever answers. That is not a loophole in the argument but the shape of the only move available, since a request by relation is an invocation wearing a question mark.

This is not a claim that blind spots are permanent. People find theirs constantly, in review, in testing, in a hallway remark that lands sideways. It is a claim about the mechanism of the finding. What you can do is invoke a source, point the source at the work, and accept whatever comes back. Invoking is a different operation from specifying. A specification is satisfied by whatever matches it, so its output is bounded by its author. An invocation is satisfied by having run, and what the invocation returns was never under your description. Every blind spot ever found was found this way, by a source that was set running rather than told what to deliver.

Four sources can supply a view you could not have reached, and three of them carry content. Different priors, another mind that can represent what yours cannot, are the only source that changes what is reachable in principle, and the source most resistant to request, so their contribution tends to ride in on the side of an exchange, unasked. A procedure such as consider-the-opposite redistributes attention inside a space you already span, mechanizes completely, and cannot surface what the space does not contain. A re-representation moves possibilities across the boundary of what is reachable in practice, which is where most decisions get made. [Where a Second Opinion Comes From]({{ '/where-a-second-opinion-comes-from/' | relative_url }}) takes the three apart and says what each one changes. What matters here is the fourth.

The fourth source supplies no content at all. The trigger is whatever causes one of the other three to run now, on this problem, rather than in general. It gates all three, since a mind never consulted, a procedure never executed, and a diagram never drawn contribute nothing. I will come back to it, because it ends up carrying more weight than the three it gates.

The core result bears directly on specification. A requirements document is incomplete, and the incompleteness does not come from insufficient effort. Completeness would require enumerating what nobody in the room has conceived, and enumeration is exactly the operation the result rules out. More elicitation raises the floor, but it does not close the gap. The distinction worth insisting on is between elicitation, which retrieves what someone in the room can represent but has not yet said, and discovery, which produces what nobody in the room represented. Interviews, workshops, story mapping, the whole apparatus of requirements practice, all of it is elicitation, and elicitation techniques are routinely sold as discovery. They are excellent at the first job. Selling them as the second sets an expectation the mechanism cannot meet, and the shortfall then gets billed to effort, to the analyst, to anything but the operation itself. The distinction is an instance of one a working paper of mine develops, between contributions that supply evidence inside a frame and contributions that replace the frame ([grounds, frames, standing](https://jongsun.dev/papers/grounds-frames-standing/)).

The same ceiling sits over a newer habit. Telling an agent to ask clarifying questions before it starts is good practice, and the questions come back sharp. They also come back from inside the space the brief spans, because a clarifying question is itself a request, it specifies what it seeks, and it is composed from the brief and from what its composer already represents. The questions sweep the known space more finely, but they never leave it. Finer sweeps are worth having, since they catch the elicitation failures, the things someone could have said and had not yet said. What they retrieve is the unsaid, not the unconceived, and a workflow that treats the two as one has quietly promised discovery while budgeting for retrieval.

That leaves the trigger. The three content-bearing sources mechanize, each in its own way. Other minds can be put on a schedule, consider-the-opposite can be written into a standing prompt, and a re-representation is close to a compilation step. Deciding that this problem, now, warrants running one of them does not mechanize, or at least nobody has managed it. The self-issued version of that decision fails exactly when confidence is highest, and highest confidence is the condition it was written for. The instruction to seek what you cannot specify gets skipped on the occasions that most need it, by the person it was meant to protect, in the state it was meant to protect against. A better question was never the remedy, because a better question searches the space you have. There is at least a countable check on whether the arranging worked. Before invoking a source, count the courses of action open to you, and count again afterwards. Not the phrasings, the courses: an option is something that changes what you would do next. If the count is unchanged, what came back was a redescription of what you already had, which is what a procedure returns and is worth having on its terms. If the count rose, something arrived that no request of yours could have named, and it arrived because you invoked rather than specified.

What remains is to arrange for the unrequested to reach you, and to notice, against the grain of your own certainty, when the arranging is due. The arranging can be delegated. The noticing, so far, cannot.
