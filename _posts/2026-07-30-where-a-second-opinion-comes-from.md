*Agreement obtained inside the frame that produced the first answer is not confirmation. Four sources of a differing view, sorted by what each one changes, and what no amount of asking reaches.*

You are unsure about a decision, so you ask the model again, in the same conversation, and you phrase the question as a challenge. Are you sure. What am I missing. Steelman the alternative. The model takes the challenge seriously, works through the considerations, and agrees with what you already had. You feel confirmed, and the feeling has some basis, because a competent reasoner has just re-examined your call and endorsed it. Nothing about the exchange was a second opinion.

Two conditions make a second view worth having. The second view must be able to see something the first could not, and the second view must not already stand inside the first one's frame. The ask-again move can pass the first condition, since the responder is capable of representing considerations you never raised. It fails the second condition every time, and the failure has nothing to do with the responder. Twenty turns into a conversation, the exchange has built a frame: a settled statement of what the problem is, a vocabulary that carves the options one particular way, a set of alternatives that were dismissed early and have silently become unmentionable. A challenge posed inside that frame gets answered inside that frame. And the narrowing is not one-sided. It is tempting to picture
yourself as the fixed point and the model as the thing that drifted toward you, but you
spent those turns accumulating commitments too, and the challenge you typed was composed
from inside the frame it was meant to test. That makes it harder to pose well, but not
impossible, and people do break out of their own frames. Breaking out repairs only the asking
half, though. The answer still comes back from inside the frame the conversation has built,
which is why self-challenge fails quietly rather than visibly.

The property at stake is decorrelation, and it is a claim about position, but not about instance count, and not even about identity. A second instance of the same model, invited into the same thread, occupies another seat inside the loop, and on the second condition it adds nothing. A fresh instance with no shared history stands outside the loop in one respect: it has not absorbed the conversation's commitments, though it still carries the first instance's priors. A model of different lineage stands outside in another respect: it brings different priors, though hand it the full thread and it inherits the frame along with everything else. Position and lineage are separate axes, and the ask-again move sits at the origin of both.

Four sources can supply a view that differs from yours, and they differ in what they change.

The first is different priors: another mind, or another model, that can represent what yours cannot. Economists have formalized the difference between learning within a fixed set of possibilities and becoming aware of possibilities you had never represented. Karni and Vierø (2013) call the second process growing awareness, and one of their results is that when the space of possibilities expands, the relative likelihoods you assigned to the old possibilities survive the expansion. In plain terms, discovering a hypothesis you had not conceived of does not reshuffle your existing beliefs, it adds to what those beliefs are about. Only this source changes what is reachable in principle. Every other source works inside a space you already span.

This source is not a second opinion at all. Asking for a second opinion presupposes that the option set is settled and only the verdict is open, which is what makes the request legible: here is my answer, tell me whether it holds. Awareness expansion has a different shape. A contribution that adds a possibility you had not represented is not a verdict on your answer, it is a report that the question was carved too small, and you cannot request it by name, because naming it would require already representing the thing you are missing. The request scopes itself to what you can already conceive, and picks this source up only by accident, when the responder volunteers something the question did not ask for. What cannot be specified is the content, though. The relation can be: asking for the reading your framing forecloses names no possibility while demanding one, and moves the representing onto whoever answers. That is the only shape the move has. It describes no output and only sets a responder
running, which is why it reads as an invocation rather than a request.

The second source is a procedure. Consider the opposite is an established debiasing instruction in psychology, and forced enumeration and structured dissent are its institutional cousins. A procedure redistributes attention within what you could already represent, and that is not a small contribution, because most misses are not an inability to conceive the alternative but a failure to spend a minute on it. Procedures are powerful, cheap, and copyable, and they are bounded above by your own space. A method cannot surface what is not in there.

The third source is a re-representation. Redraw the problem. Change the notation, put the prose in a table, draw the state machine the paragraphs were gesturing at. Nothing new enters in principle, and yet the table has a hole in it that the prose did not, because an empty cell is visible in a way a missing sentence never was. Re-representation moves possibilities across the boundary of what is reachable in practice rather than in principle, and in practice is where most decisions live.

The fourth source supplies no content at all. The trigger is whatever causes any of the first three to happen now, on this decision, rather than in general. It gates all three, since a prior you never consult, a procedure you never run, and a diagram you never draw contribute exactly nothing.

The first three sources are mechanizable in their content. Fresh instances can be spawned, models of other lineages can be queried, consider-the-opposite can be written into a standing prompt, and re-representation is close to a compilation step. The trigger is not, or at least nobody has done it. Deciding that this decision, out of the day's hundred, is the one that warrants a second view is a judgment, and the self-issued version of that judgment fails reliably. The instruction to consider more options, given to yourself, is the one you skip when you are most sure, and being most sure is the exact condition it was written for. Standing rules help at the margins, review before merge, red-team before launch, but a standing rule fires on a schedule rather than on your confidence, and misplaced confidence is the thing being defended against.

The current enthusiasm for councils of agents runs into these limits, and one more. People are building panels of models that check each other: critics reviewing generators, arbiters ranking drafts, dissenting seats written into the workflow. As decorrelation, the construction works. A council can hold seats outside your loop on both axes, fresh instances on one, other lineages on the other, and it can run the procedures and the re-representations without being asked twice. It is weakest at the source hardest to ask for, since a panel drawn from one lineage shares a space, and seats that share a space can trade verdicts all day without widening it.

As accountability, the construction does nothing. A committee of reviewers who cannot be worse off if the answer is wrong supplies scrutiny without stakes. A working paper of mine develops the distinction between interventions that supply evidence, interventions that supply a frame, and interventions that supply only authority ([grounds, frames, standing](https://jongsun.dev/papers/grounds-frames-standing/)), and the sign-off of a stakeless committee drifts toward the third kind: it changes what you feel entitled to believe without changing what anyone answers for. Adding members lengthens the chain of review. It does not terminate the chain in anyone. Human checks and balances get invoked as the precedent here, and the precedent cuts the other way. Separation of powers works because each branch is separately answerable to somebody outside the arrangement, to voters, to courts, to the removal process, and the analogy to a council of models fails exactly where the answerability does.

None of this argues against the councils. Scrutiny without stakes is still scrutiny, and a seat outside the loop is worth buying on any decision that clears the trigger. The argument is about what the councils leave in place. Three things have not moved: the source you cannot request by name, the judgment that this is the moment to seek any of them, and the answering for the decision once every opinion is in. All three still belong to whoever was unsure in the first place. Asking the same conversation one more time hands off none of them, however good the answer feels.
